## 1. Mandatory electronic invoicing (TTN / El Fatoora), Finance Laws 2025 and 2026

### Takeaway
E-invoicing is the single largest forced-purchase event of 2026: Article 53 of the Finance Law 2026 (Law 2025-17) extended the El Fatoora obligation to all service providers from 1 January 2026, an estimated 300,000+ taxpayers, with per-invoice fines. The rollout was not ready on the ground: enforcement has been softened by a tolerance for firms with a pending TTN file, sanctions' effective application is disputed, and TTN only launched a simplified web portal for low-volume issuers on 15 September 2026. Official TTN fees are low (about 10 TND/month plus 0.190 TND/invoice), so the money is in onboarding, signature, integration and hand-holding, not in the platform fee.

### Inferences
- Segment: service providers under the real regime (liberal professions, IT/consulting firms, agencies, transporters, clinics, training centres, maintenance firms). The 300,000 figure is a projection, not an adherent count. With 87% of firms having no employee, most of the obliged population are solo professionals issuing few invoices per month, which is exactly whom TTN's September 2026 portal targets.
- Current workaround: file a TTN adhesion dossier and keep issuing paper/PDF invoices under the tolerance; delegate the problem to the external accountant.
- TTN's own low-cost portal now competes with paid "simple invoicing" SaaS for micro-issuers. The defensible paid niches are therefore: (a) ERP/Odoo/WooCommerce/custom-system connectors for firms with volume, (b) done-for-you onboarding (adhesion file, TunTrust certificate, first invoices) sold through accountants, (c) accountant-side multi-client tooling, (d) inbound e-invoice reception/archiving and reconciliation with TEJ withholding certificates.
- The vendor field is crowded with small local SaaS and content-marketing agencies that appeared in 2025–2026; their public content contradicts itself on penalties and postponement, which is itself a weakness (low trust, unclear legal accuracy).
- Legal uncertainty on sanctions means demand is deadline-elastic: a confirmed sanction date would trigger a rush; a formal postponement would deflate it.

## 2. Other compliance drivers

### Takeaway
Three further hard obligations with dates force purchases: certified cash registers for on-premise consumption businesses (1 November 2025 then 1 July 2026, generalisation to 2028), the TEJ withholding-tax platform as the sole channel from 1 January 2026, and Labour Code Law 2025-9 of 21 May 2025 (fixed-term contracts converted to permanent, labour subcontracting banned). The February 2025 cheque law is not a purchase mandate but restructured B2B trade credit. EU green rules (CBAM definitive phase from 1 January 2026) reach Tunisian exporters mainly through customer data requests.

### Inferences
- Cash registers: the buyer pool is cafés, restaurants, tea rooms, fast food, pastry shops with seating. Purchase is mandatory, supplier list is closed (homologation), so the opening for a newcomer is either becoming an approved supplier/reseller or selling what sits on top (stock, staff, loyalty, delivery integration).
- TEJ + e-invoicing + cash registers together push even micro-firms to digitise bookkeeping in 2026; accountants are the bottleneck and the natural distribution channel.
- Cheque law: post-dated cheques were Tunisia's de facto trade-credit instrument. Their collapse and the ~10% bill-of-exchange rejection rate create demand for receivables tracking, bill-of-exchange management, customer credit checks, credit insurance and factoring. This is a commercial pain, not a mandated purchase.
- Labour law: firms that used staffing/outsourcing or rolling fixed-term contracts must internalise staff, which increases payroll headcount, HR administration and contract-audit needs. Facility-services firms (cleaning, security) must reposition as genuine service providers.
- CBAM's direct Tunisian exposure is narrow (cement, steel, aluminium, fertiliser); for textile and automotive suppliers the pressure is customer-driven carbon and due-diligence data requests. Researcher background knowledge, unsourced here: olive oil is not among the commodities covered by the EU deforestation regulation, so that rule is not a meaningful driver for olive-oil exporters.

### Gaps
- Number of cafés/restaurants in Tunisia, price of approved cash registers, list/number of approved suppliers and compliance rate: not found.
- INPDP (personal-data authority) enforcement, sanctions or the status of a new data-protection law in 2025–2026: no result found.
- CNSS e-filing changes, TUNEPS pain points, ISO/food-safety/halal certification costs: not researched within the call budget; no sourced findings.
- CSRD / CSDDD pass-through to Tunisian suppliers: no Tunisia-specific evidence found.
- Staffing-agency closures or job losses attributable to Law 2025-9: no figures found.
